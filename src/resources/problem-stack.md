Open any B2B sales page in any category and you will find the same first move:
name the pain. *Your pipeline is unpredictable. Your reps waste hours on research.
Your data is stale.* All true, all recognisable — and all completely inert,
because every competitor opens exactly the same way.

There is a better model for this, credited to the sales coach Taki Moore and laid
out by Stephen Steers on *Startups For the Rest of Us*. It splits customer
problems into three levels, and almost all marketing lives at the first one.

## Level 1 — the known, said problem

What the customer will discuss openly with anyone. Everybody in the industry knows
it. It is in the trade press and on every vendor's homepage.

*"A salon owner has trouble retaining clients."*

Saying this back to a prospect proves **nothing**. You have demonstrated that you
know what industry they are in. Worse, because every competitor opens here, level-1
messaging actively makes you interchangeable — you have volunteered for the
comparison grid.

Level 1 is not useless. It is table stakes: get it wrong and you are disqualified.
Get it right and you have earned exactly the right to keep talking.

## Level 2 — the known, unsaid problem

What they know perfectly well but would only tell someone they trust — because
they are slightly embarrassed by it, or unsure whether it is normal, or because
saying it out loud has political cost.

The salon owner knows retention is bad. What they do not say out loud is that they
have no idea *which* stylists are losing the clients, and they suspect it is the one
they cannot afford to confront.

When your copy names a level-2 problem, the reaction is specific and recognisable:
**"oh, you understand me."** That is the moment a cold prospect starts reading
properly. It is also the moment the comparison grid stops mattering, because no
competitor's homepage said it.

Level-2 problems are findable, but not from firmographic data. They come from
talking to customers and — crucially — from listening for the thing said *after*
the official answer. The official answer is level 1. The sentence that starts
"honestly, the real issue is…" is level 2.

## Level 3 — the unknown, unsaid problem

The connection the prospect cannot see until you show them. Steers calls it pay
dirt, and describes the effect precisely:

> Once shown, they can't unsee it, so everything else in their business is now
> viewed through that lens.
>
> Stephen Steers

For the salon: this lack of software is not only losing you clients — it is losing
you good **stylists**, because your best ones can't prove their book is growing, and
it is quietly pushing up your rent per productive chair.

That is a different kind of sentence. It does not describe a pain the prospect
brought to the conversation. It rearranges how they see their own business, and it
makes your category the lens.

Level 3 is where genuine differentiation lives, and it is hard for the obvious
reason: it requires you to understand the customer's business better than the
customer has bothered to. That is expensive. It is also the only moat available in
messaging, because a competitor can copy your level-1 and level-2 lines off your
website in an afternoon and cannot copy an insight they have not had.

## How to find each level

**Level 1** — read three competitors' homepages. Whatever all three say, that is
level 1. Do not lead with it. Cover it somewhere so you are not disqualified, then
move on.

**Level 2** — go through your last ten customer calls and find every sentence that
began with a hedge: *honestly, to be fair, the real problem is, between us.* Those
hedges are level-2 markers. If you have no recordings, ask five current customers
one question: *what did you expect this to fix that you would not have put in
writing at the time?*

**Level 3** — take a level-2 problem and ask "and what does that cost them
somewhere else in the business?" three times in a row. The first answer is usually
still level 2. The third is often level 3, because it has crossed a departmental
boundary. Cross-boundary consequences are invisible from inside a department,
which is exactly why the prospect has not seen them.

## Why this matters more in cold outreach than anywhere else

A prospect reading your website has already chosen to be there. A prospect reading
a cold email has not, and gives you one sentence before deciding what you are.

A level-1 opener — *"most teams struggle to keep their prospect data fresh"* — is
correctly classified as a template within about four words, because it is true of
everybody and therefore addressed to nobody.

A level-2 opener is addressed to a person. A level-3 opener is the rarest thing in
an inbox: **information**. It is worth reading even if the reader never buys, and
that is the actual bar for cold outreach — not "is this persuasive" but "is this
worth the ninety seconds regardless of the outcome."

## The practical version

For each segment you sell to, write three sentences and keep them on one page:

1. The problem they will say out loud. *(Cover it. Do not lead with it.)*
2. The problem they would only admit to someone they trust. *(Lead with this.)*
3. The consequence they have not connected yet. *(Earn the meeting with this.)*

If you cannot fill in row three, you do not yet know the segment well enough to
differentiate in it — and no amount of subject-line testing will substitute. That
is a research gap, not a copywriting gap, and the fix is conversations.
